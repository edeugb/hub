How I do Product Management (PM)

My product knowledge base, specialized in SaaS B2B. Consolidated theory, cases applied to my experience (case bank), and interview prep (questions and cases).

Principles and tensions

Understand before proposing
Changing systems before knowing why they exist destroys knowledge and creates enemies. (Understanding the company).
Continuous discovery, not project-based
Regular interviews even when there's no active project. The customer's accumulated knowledge is the most valuable asset. Always discovery: with a launched product the evidence is already there and the sources are discovery opportunities, tickets, usage, sales, churn — you just have to group it. For new opportunities, also chase the pains: customer interviews, clustering pains and the most frequent words in sales calls, processes that generate the most rework and wasted time or the most legal problems (and therefore more $). Make your assumptions explicit, rank them by risk, and test the riskiest one with a RAT, not an MVP. Strategy, verticals, chase the pains.
The pressure to deliver always competes with the time needed to understand well
Bad discovery doesn't save time — it shifts it into rework, into features nobody uses, and into product debt that creates risk later. Where's the balance? What are the strategies here? Continuous, lightweight discovery: short but regular interviews, agile synthesis, the OST as a living document, not a one-off artifact.
Translate everything into the MRR lever or north star metric
A feature that doesn't move acquisition, expansion, or retention doesn't matter.
Every feature in SaaS B2B ends up moving one of these three levers
Acquisition: winning new customers — opening up the market, lowering purchase friction, closing gaps that lose deals. Expansion: getting current customers to pay more — upsell, cross-sell, new modules; raises NRR. Retention: keeping them from leaving — lowering churn by attacking the pain that makes a customer consider leaving.
Problem before solution — what's the pain?
The customer is the expert in their pain; designing the answer is your job. Before evaluating any idea, define the problem it solves and the evidence behind it.
Look for the pattern, not the case
One customer asking for something is a signal. Five customers with the same problem in different words is an opportunity.
Test assumptions before building
Every solution rests on assumptions. Identifying and testing the riskiest ones first is cheaper than discovering them in production.
