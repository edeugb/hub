2 · Understanding the company

This module is my checklist when joining a new company or starting a consulting engagement.

a) About the business model

  1. What is their value proposition?
  2. Do you compete on price, specialization, or quality of customer service?

b) About our customers

Because of the B2B asymmetry, understand:

  1. Who buys (economic buyer/decision-maker) ≠
  2. who uses (end user) ≠
  3. who evaluates (champion).

In B2B, value and viability risk dominates, and the buyer is not the user: the user and the buyer are typically very different people, with very different concerns, motivations, and needs. Every product decision must answer to all three.

🧠 What does "value and viability risk dominates" mean in practice?

They're two of Cagan's four risks (value, usability, feasibility, viability — see Discovery). Value risk: the product doesn't solve a pain someone is willing to pay for. Viability risk: it doesn't work for the business (sales channel, legal, compliance, pricing, brand).

In B2C it's enough for the user to love it. In B2B, since the one who uses isn't the one who signs, users can love it and the buyer still won't renew because they don't see the ROI — or the reverse: the buyer purchases and users never adopt it (shelfware), which kills the next renewal. In practice, for me it means: before building, demand evidence on all three legs — the user will use it, the buyer will pay for it, the champion can defend it — and validate willingness to pay and the business case as early as usability.

c) Understand the strategy and goals

  1. What's the current strategy? Raising the average ticket? Every feature in SaaS B2B ends up moving one or more of these three levers: Acquisition, Expansion, Retention. Of these three, which one would I be hired to move as a priority? My discovery organizes itself around that answer.
  2. Understand the most relevant industries or the ones being focused on, and why.
  3. What are the expected business outcomes, the company's most relevant metrics.

d) About the metrics

  1. What's the company's total MRR, and of the additional modules if they have any? Calculate the ARR.
  2. What's the NRR (Net Revenue Retention)?
  3. What's the churn %?
  4. What's the ARPA / ACV (the real "customer size")?
🧠 Why does ARPA / ACV matter?

Because it defines the "customer size" you're dealing with and, with it, everything else: which GTM motion fits (table in chapter 1), how much CAC you can afford, and how much customization, support, and implementation is justified per account. A $3K ACV doesn't fund consultative selling or dedicated onboarding; an $80K one does — and it also demands compliance, SLAs, and product maturity. Additionally, comparing the base's ARPA against the ACV of new deals tells you whether the ticket is going up or down.

  1. What's the average sales cycle? (~80 days typical in B2B).
🧠 Why does the sales cycle matter?

Because it sets the pace of everything: what ships today only bills in months (don't expect impact in the same quarter); a commercial experiment needs one full cycle to give a signal, so you can't judge a GTM change in a few weeks; and features promised to close deals have to be planned with that lag. It's also a direct input to CAC: longer cycles = more selling hours per deal = higher CAC and longer payback.

e) About their GTM and growth

  1. Is it sales-led or product-led, or hybrid?
🧠 Does it relate to ACV?

Yes — directly. It's the table from chapter 1: low ACV (<$5K) → pure PLG; mid ($5K–$50K) → hybrid; high (>$50K) → sales-led. That's why this question gets answered together with the ARPA/ACV one: if the declared motion doesn't match the real ticket, there's a misalignment that usually explains CAC or conversion problems — and it's a valuable first-week finding.

f) About the team

  1. What methodologies does the team already use, how do they do product today.

g) About the products

  1. Understand the products. Main product, additional modules (→ Understanding the product).
